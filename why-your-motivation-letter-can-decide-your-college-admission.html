Why Your Motivation Letter Can Decide Your College Admission

Picture two applicants. Their grades are nearly identical, both scored well on the language test, every requirement is checked off, every deadline met. There's one spot on the program. Who gets it?

The answer might surprise you: whoever wrote the more convincing one-page letter about themselves. That person isn't necessarily smarter, more talented, or academically stronger. Over 17 years in international education, my team and I have seen this play out again and again. Parents are usually shocked to learn that a motivation letter can outweigh actual grades when it comes to getting into a university abroad.

Unfair? Maybe. But that doesn't change the rules of the game. It's more useful to understand why this happens. And it's worth knowing how to turn your letter from a box-ticking exercise into your strongest argument.

Why the motivation letter matters so much

Many universities abroad don't use entrance exams the way we're used to, which leaves admissions committees with a fairly dry set of numbers: your grade average, your language test score, maybe some academic or athletic achievements.

That's where the problem starts. A strong GPA and a high language score don't make you unique. Dozens of applicants might have the same numbers, and at a competitive university it could be hundreds, if not thousands. On paper, you can look nearly identical to the application sitting right next to yours.

This is exactly where the motivation letter changes things. It's the one place where an applicant stops being a row in a spreadsheet and becomes an actual person. You get to show not just what you did, but why you did it, what drove you, how you make decisions, where you're headed.

There's a catch, though: you don't get much space or time to show any of it. When a university gets thousands of applications, especially a highly ranked one, admissions staff can't spend long on each letter. On average, a motivation letter gets about 20 to 30 seconds of attention. Everything that came before it (years of studying, tutors, exams, sleepless nights, anxious parents) gets judged in that handful of seconds.

That's why the opening matters so much. If the first paragraph doesn't grab attention, the reader starts skimming, and sometimes they don't finish at all.

Ruth Miller, dean of admissions at Princeton's Woodrow Wilson School of Public and International Affairs, put it well: a motivation letter can make up for some of a candidate's weaknesses — or just as easily tank their chances.

Who the letter can actually change things for

Here's another wrinkle: the letter doesn't matter equally for every applicant. You can roughly split candidates into three groups.

The stars. Candidates with near-perfect academic or athletic records. Their profile already speaks for itself, so the letter gets skimmed — it won't be the deciding factor either way.

Long shots. Candidates whose numbers fall well short of the requirements. Even a brilliant essay usually can't save an application like this.

Then there's the third group. It's the biggest one by far: solid, ordinary applicants with good but not spectacular results. They're not failing academically, but they don't have an obvious edge over anyone else either.

This is where the real competition happens. Say several of these candidates have nearly identical scores and are fighting for the same spot. Once the numbers stop being useful for making a decision, the committee has to dig deeper — and that's when the motivation letters get real attention. Who told their story more convincingly? Who explained their choice with an actual story instead of nice-sounding phrases? Who showed their strengths without just listing them?

A motivation letter matters least for people who'd get in anyway, and it can't do much for people who don't meet the basic requirements. But for a solid, average-strong candidate, it can be the deciding factor.

The hook: what grabs a committee in the first few seconds

A good motivation letter has something people often call "the hook" — one or two opening lines that immediately tell the reader: this isn't another cookie-cutter dream-school essay, this is a real person with a real story. The goal isn't to sound impressive. A simple, honest, specific detail works far better.

"I've been interested in engineering since childhood" is a cliché. The committee has read that sentence hundreds of times.

Here's an alternative — something one of our students actually wrote: "As a kid, I took apart and rebuilt old electronic circuits as a hobby, and I quickly realized what interested me wasn't the gadget itself, but understanding how it worked."

The difference looks small, but the effect is completely different. The first version is a vague claim. The second gives you an actual kid, doing an actual thing. A person shows up.

That's the whole job of the hook: in the first few seconds, leave the reader with an image that's easier to remember than another "I'm driven, communicative, and motivated." From there, you can build the rest of the letter in order — education, experience, interests, achievements, plans, why this specific program — but the opening has to give the reader a reason to keep going.

A good hook works like a movie trailer: it doesn't give away the plot, but it leaves enough intrigue that you want to see the rest. Without one, a letter turns into a dry biography: born, studied, got good grades, did some projects, decided to apply... That's forgettable, and fast.

Three of the biggest mistakes in motivation letters

There's a much longer list of mistakes people make, but three of them do the most damage — often before the committee even reaches the parts that actually matter.

1. Rewriting the resume. Imagine the committee already read your CV, then opens your motivation letter and finds the same list of achievements, just restructured. What's the point? That's a wasted chance to make an impression. Your resume already tells them what you did. The letter needs to explain something else: why you did it, what kind of person is behind those achievements, what you actually learned from the experience.

2. Writing one universal letter. The temptation is huge — write it once, swap out the university name, and send it to ten more places. Convenient, but strategically a bad move.

An experienced admissions committee spots the lack of specifics fast. Where's anything about this particular program? Why these specific professors? What actually drew you to this university? Why does this program fit your plans? Without answers, the letter starts reading like "I don't care where, I just want in somewhere." Universities want the opposite: proof that they were a deliberate choice, not a random one. Everyone wants to feel special.

3. Vague motivation and stock phrases. "I want to get an interesting experience." Sounds fine at first, but ask a few follow-up questions and it falls apart: what experience? Why do you need it specifically? What are you going to do with it? Where is it supposed to lead? The same problem shows up with generic lines like "I'm a good communicator," "I handle stress well," or "I learn fast" — without a real story behind them, they're basically empty.

One more thing worth remembering on its own: don't write that your main reason for going is to "learn the language." To a committee, that can read as if the actual education is an afterthought for you.

A strong letter doesn't get written in one evening

Bad news for anyone who likes to leave things until the deadline: a good motivation letter can't be written in one sitting. Plan on it taking several weeks. Start with a draft, then put it away for a while and come back to it with fresh eyes, then show it to someone else and hear their honest reaction, ideally from someone who wasn't involved in writing it.

And here's where I'd specifically warn against looking for some ready-made universal formula. No AI, no template off the internet, no borrowed "perfect" essay can do the one thing that actually matters — find your own story. You can't rent a hook. It comes out of your own experience, your decisions, your odd hobbies, your failures, random events, and details that might seem insignificant at first.

What actually helps while you prepare

Questions for yourself. Not two or three obvious ones — a real brainstorm: past experiences, personal stories, interests, qualities, important decisions, career plans, why this profession, why this program. Collect everything first; you can pick the best parts later.

Examples of good and bad letters. They show you the difference between something alive and a pile of standard phrases.

An outside reader. A teacher, tutor, friend, or colleague — someone who can look at it with fresh eyes and honestly tell you where the story works and where it turns into a flat list of achievements.
